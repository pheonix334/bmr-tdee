How it works

  1. We calculate your BMR. If you enter your body fat %, we use the Katch-McArdle formula (based on your lean body mass) — the more accurate option when body composition is known. Otherwise we use Mifflin-St Jeor (age, gender, height, weight), the most validated formula for the general population.
  2. We factor in your activity level. BMR × an activity multiplier gives your TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure) — your maintenance calories. The activity comparison table shows how each level would change your number, so you can sanity-check your pick.
  3. Three targets appear. Maintenance (your TDEE), Weight Loss (−500 cal/day, roughly 0.5 kg/week), and Weight Gain (+500 cal/day).
  4. You also get BMI and ideal weight range. Calculated from the same height/weight, using four classic ideal-body-weight formulas (Hamwi, Devine, Robinson, Miller) averaged together.
  5. Pick a macro style. Choose which target to build macros around, then a split (Balanced, High Protein, or Low Carb) to see exact grams of protein, carbs, and fat.
  6. Push it to your tracker. "Use as my goal" sends your chosen calories and protein straight to the Calorie Tracker's daily goal.

These are population-level formulas, not individualized prescriptions — actual metabolism varies by individual, even with body fat % factored in.

Frequently asked questions

BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate) is the calories your body burns at complete rest. TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure) adds your activity level on top of that — TDEE is your actual maintenance calorie number.
A common approach is eating around 500 calories below your TDEE per day, which typically leads to about 0.5kg (1lb) of weight loss per week — shown as the "Weight Loss" target above.
If you don't enter body fat %, it uses Mifflin-St Jeor, one of the most widely validated formulas for estimating calorie needs. If you do enter body fat %, it switches to Katch-McArdle, which is generally more accurate since it's based on your actual lean body mass rather than a population average. Either way, individual metabolism can still vary by 10–15% from the estimate.
Katch-McArdle calculates BMR from your lean body mass (total weight minus fat mass) instead of age and gender averages. Two people with the same height, weight, age, and gender can have very different metabolisms if one carries more muscle — Katch-McArdle accounts for that difference, which is why it's considered more accurate when you know your body fat percentage.
It's an estimated healthy weight range based on your height, calculated using four classic medical formulas (Hamwi, Devine, Robinson, and Miller) that predate BMI. They're population averages from decades-old studies, not individual targets — someone with more muscle mass than average will reasonably weigh more than these ranges suggest.
